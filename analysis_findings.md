# Analysis Findings

## Research Question

**Did wage growth keep pace with inflation in the United States after 2020?**

---

## Hypothesis

Wages increased after 2020, but inflation temporarily outpaced wage growth during the 2021–2022 inflation surge, slowing the growth of workers' real purchasing power.

---

## Data Sources

This analysis uses two monthly FRED series:

- **CPIAUCSL** — Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers: All Items in U.S. City Average
- **AHETPI** — Average Hourly Earnings of Production and Nonsupervisory Employees, Total Private

Analysis period:

**January 2020 – August 2026**

---

## Key Findings

### 1. Inflation surged sharply after 2020

U.S. inflation accelerated significantly during 2021 and reached its highest year-over-year rate in the dataset in:

**June 2022 — 8.98%**

This represented a substantial increase in the rate at which consumer prices were rising.

---

### 2. Wage growth increased, but temporarily lagged inflation

Wages also increased during the period, but wage growth did not keep pace with inflation during the peak of the inflation surge.

In **June 2022**:

| Measure | Value |
|---|---:|
| Inflation | 8.98% |
| Wage Growth | 6.58% |
| Wage–Inflation Gap | -2.40 percentage points |

The negative gap indicates that consumer prices were increasing faster than average hourly earnings at that point.

---

### 3. Real hourly earnings weakened during the inflation surge

To examine purchasing power, nominal hourly earnings were adjusted using CPI and expressed in January 2020 purchasing-power terms.

A real earnings index was then created:

**January 2020 = 100**

Real hourly earnings reached their lowest point after 2020 in:

**June 2022 — 101.12**

This means real hourly earnings declined from their earlier post-2020 levels during the inflation surge.

However, the index remained above the January 2020 baseline of 100.

Therefore, the data does **not** support the conclusion that real hourly earnings fell below their January 2020 level.

---

### 4. Real earnings recovered after the inflation surge

Following the 2022 low, real hourly earnings generally recovered.

By 2025–2026, the real earnings index was approximately **105–106**, compared with the January 2020 baseline of 100.

This suggests that average hourly earnings, after adjusting for changes in consumer prices, were above their January 2020 level by the end of the analysis period.

---

## Overall Conclusion

The data provides evidence that **wage growth did not keep pace with inflation during the 2021–2022 inflation surge**.

Inflation peaked at approximately **8.98% in June 2022**, while wage growth was only **6.58%**, creating a **2.40 percentage-point gap**.

This temporarily weakened real hourly earnings, which reached a post-2020 low of **101.12** in June 2022 when January 2020 is set to 100.

However, real hourly earnings remained above the January 2020 baseline and subsequently recovered, reaching approximately **105–106** in 2026.

### Main takeaway

> **Inflation temporarily outpaced wage growth after 2020, weakening real purchasing power during the 2021–2022 surge. However, real hourly earnings later recovered and remained above their January 2020 level.**

## Important Interpretation Notes

- CPI is a measure of the consumer price level. Inflation is calculated as the year-over-year percentage change in CPI.
- Wage growth is calculated as the year-over-year percentage change in average hourly earnings.
- The real earnings calculation adjusts nominal hourly earnings for changes in CPI.
- The real earnings index uses January 2020 as the baseline (100).
- The analysis focuses on average hourly earnings for **production and nonsupervisory employees in the private sector**, not all U.S. workers.
- A negative wage–inflation gap indicates that prices were growing faster than wages during that period.
- A negative wage–inflation gap does not necessarily mean real earnings are below the January 2020 level; it indicates that prices were increasing faster than wages relative to the previous year.
- October 2025 contains a missing CPI observation. The missing value is retained as missing rather than being estimated or imputed.
